# Solidarity at scale

## Personal relationships, cultural symbols, and institutions in political cooperation

Research manuscript prepared for Nolan Downard. Version 0.1, 27 September 2026, America/Los_Angeles. Targeted critical synthesis and elementary formal models. This manuscript has not been peer reviewed. No original human-participant study or new meta-analysis was conducted.

## Abstract

What enables solidarity to extend beyond personal acquaintance? This paper distinguishes demographic similarity, perceived familiarity, symbolic competence, shared interests, and institutional reliability. A targeted evidence map combines psychological research, field experiments, political-economic studies, and historical texts. The evidence favors a conditional account: identities can help people anticipate cooperation, but collaboration, incentives, networks, and institutional arrangements influence whether this becomes sustained collective action. Neither ethnic uniformity nor the disappearance of particular identities is a demonstrated prerequisite. Historical comparison reveals that autonomous organization and wider coalition-building can coexist. Under commitments to equal rights, protection from domination, and material inclusion, the provisional recommendation is to combine protected group voice with cross-group collaboration and accountable institutions. The complete combination has not been established as a universally superior intervention. Elementary Lean models distinguish local contact limits from network reach and characterize cooperation in a specified donation game. They establish conditional mathematical implications, not empirical necessity or political legitimacy. Remaining uncertainties concern durability, transfer across settings, institutional capture, and the comparative effects of constitutional arrangements.

## Introduction

A person can belong to a city, region, nation, profession, religious community, and musical scene at the same time. Some memberships nest; others intersect. Their coexistence raises a useful question: what changes when cooperation extends from people we know to people we recognize only as fellow members?

The motivating intuition is that small groups cooperate through similarity and that larger groups must somehow reproduce that similarity. The intuition identifies a real coordination problem but leaves its proposed solution underspecified. People may resemble one another in ancestry while disagreeing about resources. They may understand a shared story without trusting its teller. Conversely, strangers may cooperate through enforceable agreements without sharing a biography. The question therefore becomes which dimensions must align, for which tasks, under which institutions.

We distinguish three outcomes that are often treated as interchangeable. Coordination means fitting actions together. Cooperation includes bearing a cost to benefit others or a collective undertaking. Solidarity adds an interpretation of mutual obligation and can motivate action beyond immediate exchange. An exploitative organization may coordinate efficiently. A friendly community may fail to provide public goods. Effective domination is not a successful outcome under an egalitarian objective.

## Methods and scope

This is a purposive rapid evidence map followed by critical synthesis. Initial discovery began from the project conversation and named works. Searches expanded through web search, publisher and university records, archival texts, and PubMed metadata. Exact new queries and access failures are logged in the accompanying project. Sources were selected to discriminate among mechanisms, correct historical identifications, or challenge the initial hypothesis. English-language availability shaped inclusion. The review is neither preregistered nor exhaustive; no claim is made that every eligible study was screened.

Research reports, theoretical proposals, historical declarations, and normative arguments were analyzed in separate roles. A declaration documents an articulated program; it does not independently identify its effects. Experimental assignment supports a narrower causal claim than an unrestricted generalization to nations. Abstract-only records are marked. The evidence register records access depth and locators. No pooled effect was calculated because the selected outcomes and designs are not commensurable. Published effects below are attributed to their original studies.

Confidence terms describe provisional judgments about specified claims: high for directly checked textual or mathematical statements; moderate for converging but bounded mechanism evidence; low for extrapolation across scales; unresolved when the relevant comparison was not identified. These are not formal GRADE ratings.

## A framework for scaling

Four dimensions help separate levels of analysis. Relational reach concerns who can communicate or reciprocate. Cultural interpretation concerns the meanings participants attach to signals. Material interdependence concerns who gains or loses from action. Institutional organization concerns rules, authority, dispute resolution, and accountability. Increasing population may change all four without preserving the same mechanism.

Durkheim's distinction between solidarity through likeness and through differentiated interdependence is an early reason to question the necessity of increasing sameness [S14]. It is a conceptual distinction, not a finding that any division of labor automatically produces fair cooperation. Differences can support complementary roles while simultaneously sustaining unequal power.

Administrative nesting also does not prove self-similarity. A municipality and a nation can both have representatives while differing fundamentally in fiscal capacity, coercive authority, and membership rules. A rigorous self-similarity claim would specify a transformation across scales and demonstrate invariant relationships under it. The present project does not establish such invariance.

## Evidence on relationships and symbols

The approximately 150-contact claim should not be a fixed premise. Lindenfors and colleagues challenged the precision of estimates extrapolated from primate brain data [S01]. Mac Carron and colleagues found layered structure in telephone contacts [S02]. Those propositions can coexist: evidence for structured social attention is not evidence for one universal population ceiling. Direct relationships and the total reach of a connected network are different quantities.

The idea that a stranger evokes someone close to us has a relevant theoretical home in Andersen and Chen's relational-self account [S05]. It supplies a candidate mechanism for interpersonal responses, not proof that large coalitions are constructed by resemblance to a person's family. The sign of the response may depend on the relationship evoked. Familiarity should therefore be measured separately from positive evaluation.

Efferson and colleagues experimentally studied how initially arbitrary markers can acquire behavioral meaning in coordination [S04]. The important distinction is between sharing a visible label and sharing reliable expectations. An emblem can identify a partner, an aspiration, a fashion, or a deliberate imitation; these uses have different implications. A research design should test comprehension, affiliation, and expected reciprocity separately.

Roccas and Brewer formulated social identity complexity in terms of perceived relations among memberships [S28]. Miller and colleagues investigated its correlates and antecedents [S06]. This helps frame multiple identities without treating one identity as the person's essence. The retrieved evidence does not establish that increasing an identity-complexity score necessarily improves costly cooperation in all settings.

## Cooperation across groups

Balliet and colleagues reported a standardized in-group cooperation advantage of d = 0.32, with d = 0.19 in settings without mutual interdependence and d = 0.42 in interdependent settings [S03]. These are standardized differences, not percentage increases. The evidence register preserves their abstract-level provenance; the present project did not reanalyze the meta-analysis. The findings motivate attention to expected reciprocity as well as categorization.

An experimental-games study involving 300 participants in Kampala favored explanations involving cooperative expectations and social enforcement over simple shared tastes or greater altruism toward co-ethnics [S07]. It does not identify the causal effect of changing an entire community's ethnic composition. A Sierra Leone analysis instead found no adverse association between local diversity and several public-goods outcomes, including an instrumental-variable specification [S12]. Its identification still depends on the instrument and historical controls. Together these studies weaken any context-free rule that diversity necessarily prevents collective provision.

Mousa's randomized soccer intervention in post-ISIS Iraq improved several behaviors toward Muslim peers among Christian participants, while broader off-field outcomes did not show the same gains [S08]. Lowe's cricket experiment in India distinguished beneficial collaborative contact from generally adverse competitive contact [S09]. These studies support designing relationships around common tasks, but neither is a direct trial of a national political order. Paluck and colleagues' review of randomized contact interventions with delayed outcomes further emphasizes uneven coverage and small-study concerns [S10].

The institutional alternative also needs skepticism. Casey and colleagues found short-run public-goods and economic improvements from a Sierra Leone development program without evidence of durable change in the broader institutional outcomes studied [S11]. Ostrom's analysis of polycentric governance describes how authority and tasks can be distributed across levels [S13]. The conjunction suggests a design question, not an institutional cure-all: which arrangements actually change incentives, distribute authority, and survive beyond a supported intervention?

## Historical comparison without false equivalence

Washington's 1895 address offered economic cooperation alongside social separation; editorial historical evidence also documents private support for legal challenges [S15]. Du Bois's 1903 criticism demanded voting rights, civic equality, and appropriate education [S16]. This is a conflict over rights, strategy, and political opportunity, not simply business versus intellectual improvement. Their writings cannot identify the counterfactual effects of replacing one strategy with another under otherwise identical conditions. Du Bois’s 1919 report of the Pan-African Congress adds a transnational organizational setting to this early comparison [S35]; it does not supply a complete account of his later development.

Malcolm X's 1964 OAAU founding speech combined independent Black organization with international linkage [S17]. King's Poor People's Campaign proposed multiracial economic mobilization; the historical record also documents constrained results [S18]. Malcolm’s April 1964 speech connects community control with electoral and international demands [S33]. King’s 1967 antiwar address links domestic poverty and racism with obligations beyond nationality [S32]. Reading dated statements helps avoid treating either thinker as a timeless, internally uniform philosophy. In the cases considered here, autonomy and wider cooperation are organizational choices that can be combined or sequenced.

The Combahee River Collective's statement articulates specific group oppression alongside coalition commitments and criticism of separatism [S19]. Baker's organizing legacy, documented by the SNCC Digital Gateway, shifts attention toward distributed grassroots capacity [S31]. These sources challenge a genealogy restricted to prominent male leaders and make internal voice part of effectiveness rather than an afterthought.

Nkrumah's argument for continental economic coordination also explicitly recognizes uneven benefits among states [S21]. Getachew's introduction places anticolonial projects within an attempted reconstruction of international order [S29]. These readings undermine the assumption that Pan-Africanism can be exhausted by racial resemblance or that nationalism is invariably the opposite of internationalism. They do not settle which federation would have succeeded.

The Balfour Declaration is a different kind of object: an imperial government's statement concerning a national home in a territory inhabited by others, with specified protections in its wording [S20]. It is not equivalent to an advocacy association or Du Bois's equal-citizenship argument. Comparing these cases requires explicit treatment of territory, consent, displacement, citizenship, and coercive power. This review does not estimate comparative outcomes for Israeli constitutional alternatives, Japan, or either Korean state.

## Culture and contemporary political arguments

The oral form the project initially sought is the African American narrative toast. Jackson's research is discussed in a university interview [S22]. Artist interviews document Rudy Ray Moore's influence on particular rappers [S23]. This is a useful case of transmission through performance and media, but it cannot establish an aggregate increase in cultural homogeneity or political unity. Reception, ownership, gendered content, and institutional organization require distinct evidence.

Contemporary disagreement clarifies the evaluative stakes. Appiah's discussion of cosmopolitanism allows universal concern alongside particular attachments [S26]. Fraser frames recognition and redistribution as jointly necessary dimensions of justice [S30]. Reed's essay foregrounds political-economic explanation and challenges essentialized accounts of racial politics [S24]. These are arguments to assess, not experimental effect estimates. Taiwo's Elite Capture is retained as a relevant reading lead; the full book was not assessed here [S25].

The strongest objection to a broad material-interest coalition is that universal language can conceal whose interests are being represented. The strongest objection to identity-centered organization is that leaders can claim to speak for a diverse population while insulating themselves from accountability. Neither objection establishes that its target must fail. Both suggest asking who selects representatives, controls resources, sets priorities, and can contest decisions.

## Mobility and colonial power as separate questions

The proposal for freedom to move and work raises both moral and empirical questions. Carens’s argument for open borders proceeds through political philosophy [S34]. It cannot by itself estimate effects on wages, fiscal systems, representation, or security. A useful empirical comparison would specify which rights move with a person, whether employers can exploit status differences, and how affected communities contest decisions.

Free movement also does not logically guarantee the disappearance of group-specific needs. People could move freely while unequal wealth or unequal treatment persists. Conversely, retaining a cultural association need not require territorial exclusion. The hypothesis to test is whether particular mobility and citizenship arrangements reduce domination while preserving effective voice, not whether populations inevitably become one race. Racial categorization, ancestry, and culture should be modeled as distinct, historically variable concepts.

The colonial counterfactual needs equal care. Africa is a continent with multiple political histories. An economic account of extraction and an account of racial domination can describe interacting mechanisms. The slave-trade-and-trust literature supplies a relevant but distinct historical question [S27]; it cannot stand in for an explanation of nineteenth-century partition. To ask whether an alternative coalition could have prevented conquest, specify when it could have formed, its resources, internal bargaining, military and financial asymmetries, and the likely response of imperial powers. The sources reviewed here do not identify that counterfactual.

## Formal models and their interpretation

The Lean development first uses the infinite path on natural numbers. Every vertex has neighbors contained in two possible positions, yet every pair of vertices is reachable by a finite path. Thus a bound on immediate contacts does not logically bound total connected population. This result says nothing about communication delay, trust decay, or cognitive capacity. A long chain is a counterexample to a claimed mathematical necessity, not a blueprint for social organization.

Membership predicates then give explicit examples of nested and overlapping affiliations. A separate witness assigns no trust to a connected population. The simplicity of these results is deliberate: they expose assumptions that an informal argument might otherwise smuggle into the word group.

The behavioral model is a symmetric two-player donation game. A contribution gives the other player benefit b and costs its contributor c. A reliable external institution charges every defector sanction s. Payoffs use integer tokens; negative tokens are permitted. The matrix for the row player is:

| Row action | Other contributes | Other defects |
|---|---|---|
| Contribute | b - c | -c |
| Defect | b - s | -s |

Mutual contribution resists unilateral deviation exactly when c <= s. With positive costs and no sanctions it does not. Concrete witnesses exhibit heterogeneous labels with stable contribution and identical labels without it. These results rely on attributes not entering payoffs, full enforcement, fixed costs, and a specified deviation concept. They establish possibility and non-implication only. They do not prove labels never matter, justify coercion, or address the cost and legitimacy of enforcement. The accompanying verification report records actual build status and axiom output.

## A conditional political verdict

The strongest descriptive conclusion is that scaling changes the means of coordination; it need not reproduce demographic sameness. The strongest practical mechanism recommendation is to organize repeated collaboration around concrete shared outcomes while making expectations and accountability credible. The political recommendation is weaker because it combines evidence from distinct levels rather than a direct comparative trial.

Under equal civic standing, protection from domination, and material inclusion as evaluative commitments, the most defensible working design is a pluralist coalition: protect autonomous group voice, negotiate shared material goals, distribute authority where local knowledge matters, and maintain enforceable protections against abuse within and between groups. This is a conditional synthesis, not a discovered universally optimal regime.

Its sequence matters. First protect participation and address immediate threats. Next establish representative procedures and a specific common project. Then create repeated opportunities for successful collaboration, transparent resource allocation, dispute resolution, and independent assessment. Expand only after observing retention, distribution of benefits, and treatment of dissent. This sequence is proposed for evaluation, not presented as an experimentally validated package.

A security emergency may make autonomous organization temporarily indispensable. A redistribution campaign may require a constituency wider than any single ethnic group. A service-delivery network may benefit from federated administration. Territorial self-determination demands a separate analysis of affected populations and feasible alternatives. There is no evidence-based shortcut from successful sports contact to an answer about borders.

The recommendation would weaken if representative coalition structures repeatedly failed while a feasible alternative improved both rights and material outcomes without exporting harm. It would strengthen through replicated field comparisons with sustained behavioral outcomes, credible identification, and evidence of transfer across settings. A nominally inclusive organization that suppresses internal dissent would fail the stated criteria even if it mobilized efficiently.

## Limitations and research agenda

The source selection was targeted and English-dominant, with no independent second reviewer. Several psychological sources were available only as abstracts. Historical comparisons are illustrative, not matched-case causal estimates. Broad policy questions about migration, statehood, and international redistribution remain underidentified. The evidence does not support a new meta-analysis or precise comparative ranking of whole ideologies.

The next empirical test should separate symbolic comprehension from material interdependence and institutional reliability, measure actual costly behavior, and test whether effects persist with unfamiliar partners. A later consented study can examine significant-other resemblance independently. The project protocol specifies these studies and their limitations. Contemporary theories and less accessible historical materials require deeper primary-text work before a publishable review could claim adequate coverage.

## Conclusion

The evidence supports cooperation through shared expectations, complementary interests, and institutional arrangements without establishing demographic homogeneity as a prerequisite. Personal familiarity and cultural recognition are plausible contributors rather than universal foundations. Political evaluation must add distribution, rights, and accountability to mobilization success. The resulting recommendation favors protected pluralism and cross-group collaboration under stated commitments, while leaving comparative state design and long-run political superiority unresolved.
