# Solidarity at scale

## Evidence and political decision report

Prepared for Nolan Downard. Initial research version, 27 September 2026, America/Los_Angeles. Companion to the manuscript, source register, and Lean models. This is a targeted evidence map, not a completed systematic review.

## 0 Executive decision brief

**Main answer.** Large-scale solidarity need not depend on making everyone alike. People can cooperate through recognizable commitments, shared tasks, complementary interests, and trustworthy procedures. The evidence does not identify a universal size limit, a necessary degree of ethnic homogeneity, or one best political ideology.

**Political recommendation.** Given equal rights, non-domination, and broadly shared material gains as goals, use a pluralist coalition with protected group voice and specific common projects. Build accountable representation and assess actual distribution of benefits. Treat this as a provisional design recommendation. Evidence for its constituent mechanisms is stronger than evidence that the combined package outperforms every alternative.

**Scope and confidence.** Psychological and field evidence informs mechanisms; historical texts clarify programs; mathematical models check implications. Moderate confidence supports context-dependent identity and cooperation mechanisms. Low confidence supports extrapolation to national institutional design. Freedom-of-movement policy and competing statehood arrangements require additional evidence. The bands are adapted judgments, not formal GRADE ratings.

**Five findings with direct consequences.**

1. In-group cooperation advantage is real on average in a published synthesis: d = 0.32, not a 32 percent increase [S03]. Measure its conditions rather than treating ancestry as destiny.
2. Collaboration and competition across groups can have different effects [S09]. Design the encounter, not just the population mix.
3. In one Iraqi soccer experiment, an on-field sportsmanship outcome rose 26 percentage points, while broader off-field transfer was limited [S08]. Test transfer rather than presuming it.
4. Local ethnic diversity was not associated with poorer public goods in a Sierra Leone study [S12]. Context-free homogeneity claims are too strong.
5. A development program improved some outputs without demonstrating durable institutional transformation [S11]. Delivering a project and changing power are separate outcomes.

**Next actions.** Complete full-text appraisal of decisive abstract-only sources; run a preregistered experiment separating cultural signals from incentives; expand the historical comparison with explicit outcome and case-selection rules. No participant recruitment has begun.

## 1 Abstract

This report translates a question about small-group similarity into a research program on cooperation across scale. It distinguishes familiarity, culture, identity, interests, and institutions; evaluates political strategies against multiple outcomes; corrects historical conflations; and defines what Lean can verify. The recommendation combines particular group voice with broader collaboration, subject to accountability and equal-rights criteria. It is an inference from heterogeneous evidence, not the result of a direct experiment comparing political systems.

## 2 Introduction

Your original question contains an especially useful distinction: someone may feel familiar because they evoke a person you know, or because they understand a reference you recognize. These cues can overlap, but neither logically requires the other. A person may share your cultural vocabulary while pursuing incompatible goals; another may know little of your background but honor a clear commitment.

The project therefore asks what must be shared to accomplish a particular form of collective action. It replaces one broad homogeneity variable with measurable dimensions. It also separates a group's ability to act from whether the action is fair to members or outsiders.

## 3 Methods

The review uses targeted searching, source retrieval, and comparative reasoning. It incorporates original studies, a published meta-analysis, a policy-oriented review, primary historical texts, scholarly interpretations, and explicitly marked discovery leads. English-language online accessibility influenced selection. Retrieval occurred on 27 September Pacific time and 28 September UTC. ## 4 Findings

### Similarity is not one thing

| Dimension | Example | What to measure |
|---|---|---|
| Demographic composition | Distribution of self-identified backgrounds | Composition and segregation, with appropriate consent |
| Perceived similarity | A stranger seems like me | Rating independent of actual background |
| Relational familiarity | Someone evokes a known person | Resemblance, relationship valence, and accessibility |
| Symbolic competence | Understanding a story or practice | Comprehension, interpretation, and endorsement separately |
| Shared expectations | Anticipating reciprocity | Incentivized predictions and observed behavior |
| Institutional reliability | Expecting agreements to be upheld | Actual reliability, contestability, and enforcement cost |

Andersen and Chen provide a relevant account of how significant-other representations enter encounters [S05]. Efferson and colleagues offer a distinct account of markers becoming useful for coordination [S04]. These are competing or interacting candidates. Their relative contributions to political coalition formation remain unmeasured here.

### Size and scale

The Dunbar literature concerns patterns and constraints on relationships, not a rule limiting the size of every organization. Competing analyses qualify the precision of 150 [S01; S02]. A person can have few direct contacts while participating in a much larger network. That does not mean communication is effortless: path length, misinformation, bottlenecks, and accountability may worsen as reach expands.

Nested identities describe containment. Cross-cutting identities describe overlap. A city can be administratively nested in a state while its residents share professional or religious identities with people elsewhere. Neither arrangement proves that local and national politics operate by the same causal law.

### Institutions and inequality

The Kampala experiments distinguish explanations based on tastes from explanations based on expectations and enforcement [S07]. Ostrom's analysis provides a framework for distributing governance across levels [S13]. Both motivate asking how cooperation is organized. Neither licenses the conclusion that more policing or more organizational layers always improves welfare.

Institutions also determine who can make demands. The relevant question is not simply whether a rule is enforced, but whether affected people can contest it and whether enforcement protects them equally. Efficiency and domination can coexist. A research project that measures only contribution rates could mistakenly recommend an abusive institution.

### Historical corrections

Washington cannot be summarized as a straightforward advocate of labor unions, and Du Bois cannot be reduced to an educational route toward separatist statehood [S15; S16]. The OAAU, a civic coalition, a transnational federation, and a sovereign state are different objects. The historical comparison file distinguishes their constituencies, authority, territorial claims, and outcome evidence.

King's economic coalition project and the Combahee statement provide examples of common aims coexisting with attention to distinct group positions [S18; S19]. The important research question becomes how coalition partners retain voice and resolve conflicts over priorities.

### Culture and political grouping

Toasts are the oral-narrative tradition relevant to the Dolemite example [S22]. Moore's influence on named artists is documented through interviews [S23]. That supports a transmission case, not a theory that everyone adopting a cultural reference becomes politically homogeneous. A shared aesthetic may cross social boundaries without eliminating them.

### Mobility and historical development

Carens’s philosophical case for open borders is a reading entry point [S34]. This project has not estimated the effects of migration arrangements. Compare residence, work, citizenship, labor protection, and representation separately; freedom to move alone does not logically eliminate group-specific harms. Dated texts also matter: Du Bois’s 1919 congress report, Malcolm’s April 1964 speech, and King’s 1967 antiwar address broaden the comparison beyond fixed labels [S35; S33; S32].

## 5 Conditional political verdict

**Most supported explanation:** cooperation depends on relationships among identity, expectations, material incentives, and institutions. A universal homogeneity explanation is less defensible than this conditional account.

**Most defensible strategy for the stated goals:** preserve group-specific voice while constructing practical collaboration across groups. Add transparent resource decisions, contestable authority, and protections for people who disagree. This is a recommendation derived from evidence and ethical premises, not an identified total treatment effect.

| Strategy | Best-fitting task | Main vulnerability | Current verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| Group-specific mutual aid | Immediate support, voice, safety | Gatekeeping and limited reach | Useful component; not a whole political order |
| Material-interest coalition | Broad bargaining over resources | Specific harms obscured by majority priorities | Promising if distribution and representation are explicit |
| Common civic identity | Shared rules and equal standing | Dominant culture presented as neutral | Useful when belonging is inclusive in practice |
| Pluralist or dual-identity coalition | Cooperation with retained affiliations | More negotiation and potential veto conflict | Preferred working design under this report's goals |
| Federated or polycentric institutions | Problems spanning different local scales | Fragmentation, unequal capacity, capture | Match authority to task; assess empirically |
| Territorial self-determination | Protection or control under specific conditions | New minorities, contested territory, displacement | Separate case analysis required; no general ranking |

The recommended sequence is to secure participation, choose a concrete common task, negotiate representation and benefit-sharing, create repeated collaboration, and monitor both success and exclusion. Expand only when the arrangement demonstrates acceptable outcomes. This proposed sequence is a researchable program, not a validated recipe.

The normative reasons are explicit: equal standing protects a person from being discounted merely because of group membership; non-domination requires contestable power; inclusion asks whether gains reach those expected to contribute. These commitments are open to philosophical challenge. The empirical literature cannot select moral premises by itself.

Changing priorities changes recommendations. If urgent protection dominates, autonomous organizing may take priority. If maximizing short-run output dominates, a centralized arrangement may be attractive even when this report would reject its rights costs. If local autonomy dominates, federation gains weight but coordination and unequal capacity need attention. No numerical composite ranking is supplied because the required outcome estimates and legitimate weights are absent.

## 6 Deconstructive analysis

The starting argument can be separated into four claims: personal networks are bounded; similarity supports cooperation; symbolic identity expands the in-group; and enlarged in-groups produce effective political coalitions. Even if the first three are sometimes true, the fourth does not follow automatically.

Each step needs a bridge. A bounded number of direct ties does not bound indirect reach. Similarity may predict opportunities to interact rather than preference for cooperation. Shared meaning may coordinate conflict as readily as mutual aid. Political effectiveness requires resources, institutions, and a feasible target. Treating all these bridges as homogeneity makes the explanation difficult to falsify.

A second conflation concerns motive and result. An organization may fail despite reasonable strategy because repression increases. A successful organization may benefit from external support unavailable to competitors. An outcome alone does not reveal a leader's hidden motive or identify what another strategy would have achieved.

## 7 Reconstructive analysis

Begin with agents who differ in knowledge, resources, and preferences. Give them opportunities to interact, noisy signals of reliability, and rules for allocating gains. Cooperation may emerge when benefits and expectations make it worthwhile, when norms support it, or when institutions reduce vulnerability. Culture can influence each element without being a fixed essence.

Add groups as overlapping memberships rather than boxes that completely determine persons. Allow representatives to diverge from constituents. Add a second governance layer only when it solves a coordination problem that lower layers cannot handle adequately. Measure costs introduced by that layer as well as benefits.

This reconstruction yields falsifiable predictions: if symbolic comprehension matters independently, it should predict behavior after goals and enforcement are held constant; if institutional guarantees substitute for familiarity, their effects should be strongest among unfamiliar partners. Interaction effects should be specified before seeing outcomes.

## 8 Middle-out synthesis

Organizations are a useful intermediate level between individuals and states. A food cooperative, workplace association, or local coalition has observable membership rules, tasks, resource allocation, and disputes. These features permit study without pretending that a two-person game directly represents a nation.

The empirical sequence should move from controlled mechanism tests to organizational comparisons, followed by carefully bounded policy inference. The mathematical sequence should move from explicit definitions to conditional results, counterexamples, and sensitivity to assumptions. The two sequences meet when a model parameter corresponds to an observed institutional mechanism; neither replaces the other.

## 12 Inference and robustness assessment

**Robustness verdict:** strongest for rejecting simple logical necessities and for bounded experimental findings; moderate for a conditional mechanism account; low for the overall political recommendation; unresolved for a universal ranking of constitutional systems.

No new meta-analysis was performed. Q, tau-squared, I-squared, pooled effects, funnel plots, and Egger tests are therefore not computed. The selected evidence combines standardized game effects, field behavior, institutional outcomes, and historical interpretation. Pooling them would conceal rather than resolve heterogeneity. Paluck and colleagues' analysis shows why even a narrower contact literature requires attention to coverage and small-study patterns [S10].

Sensitivity reasoning gives three conclusions. Remove the historical cases and the bounded experimental claims remain, but the genealogy becomes unsupported. Remove abstract-only psychological records and several proposed mechanisms become more tentative, while the field-study counterexamples remain. Remove the equal-rights and non-domination premises and the political recommendation no longer follows, even though empirical estimates are unchanged.

Formal proofs do not remove these vulnerabilities. A sanction threshold assumes reliable enforcement and omits its financing, legitimacy, and selective application. If enforcement is corrupt or asymmetric, the simplified model is not a reliable policy guide. Labels are payoff-irrelevant by construction; the existence proof cannot establish empirical irrelevance of identity.

What would change the conclusion? Repeated, credible field comparisons demonstrating sustained benefits and fair distribution from a rival arrangement would alter the recommendation. Evidence that the preferred coalition design systematically suppresses marginalized members would also count against it. Confidence intervals, meaningful-effect thresholds, and explicit value trade-offs matter more than accumulating agreeable citations.
