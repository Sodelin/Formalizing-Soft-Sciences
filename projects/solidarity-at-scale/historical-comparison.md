# Historical comparison and remaining readings

This is a comparison of retrieved, dated texts and institutional forms. It does not attribute an entire lifetime's views to one speech. Numerical minority, subordinated group, diaspora, civic association, federation, and sovereign state are distinct categories.

The original comparison between Du Bois and Zionism can be retained as a question about collective security. It becomes misleading if that shared problem is treated as proof of equivalent institutions. Compare territory, citizenship, sovereign coercion, consent, rights of nonmembers, internal representation, and the feasible alternatives available at the time. The Balfour Declaration is not itself an adequate account of the diversity of Zionist thought or of Palestinian political claims. This version does not settle those histories.

The original account of Washington also needs correction: the retrieved speech supports industrial and economic advancement, not a simple characterization as a labor-union advocate. A public compromise with separation and private support for legal challenge can coexist. Neither fact proves what he secretly believed in every setting. Du Bois’s 1903 case for rights cannot be reduced to waiting for an educated elite to win permission for equality.

The minority-coalition hypothesis should specify power rather than assuming numerical size measures disadvantage. A wealthy numerical minority, a disenfranchised majority, and a diaspora with varying citizenship statuses face different problems. A coalition may unite material interests across racial lines while still needing procedures to address distinct harms within it. The comparison tests that compatibility; it does not erase the causal role of racialization by renaming every conflict economic.

The colonial counterfactual requires separate cases: choose a place, decade, plausible coalition, available resources, and imperial opponent. Compare successful resistance, partial autonomy, defeated coalitions, and internally coercive alternatives. The slave-trade study [S27] concerns an earlier historical process and trust; it is not evidence that a specified nineteenth-century coalition would have prevented partition. Economic extraction and racialized rule should be examined as interacting mechanisms.

Future readings, not yet assessed: Du Bois’s The Talented Tenth and later economic writings; Fanon’s The Wretched of the Earth; C. L. R. James’s The Black Jacobins; Cedric Robinson’s Black Marxism; Stuart Hall on identification and articulation; Benedict Anderson on imagined communities; Fredrik Barth on ethnic boundaries; Charles Tilly on durable inequality; Iris Marion Young on difference and representation; Michael Walzer and other critics of open borders; contemporary African, Asian, Indigenous, Jewish, and Palestinian scholarship on membership and self-determination. These are a balanced expansion agenda, not an implied completed literature review.
